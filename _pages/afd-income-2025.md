Do low earners vote AfD? <br>What income tells us about AfD support

<p class="ltw-standfirst">

Support for the AfD is often linked to poverty and low incomes. Survey data from the 2025 federal election paint a different picture: AfD voting varies only weakly with household income. Formal education matters far more.

</p>

<p class="ltw-byline">

by <strong><a href="https://juliuskoelzer.de/">Julius Kölzer</a></strong> · October 2026

</p>

In public debate, support for the AfD is often traced back to poverty and low incomes. “We have long known that the AfD is particularly successful where people are poor and getting poorer,” reads a recent contribution in <a href="https://www.zeit.de/2026/24/afd-stoppen-strategien-kunst-literatur-wissenschaft#inan">DIE ZEIT</a>. In the same vein, a commentary in der Freitag headlined <a href="https://www.freitag.de/autoren/sebastian-baehr/mehr-reiche-und-mehr-arme-naehrboden-fuer-die-afd">“Managergehälter steigen, Armut nimmt zu”</a> (executive pay rises, poverty grows) argues that with rising poverty “the breeding ground for the far-right AfD is growing as well”. After the 2025 federal election, in which the AfD became the strongest party among workers, it was also frequently described as the new party of the “left behind”, and in a newspaper column the head of the German Institute for Economic Research (DIW) even speaks of an <a href="https://www.diw.de/de/diw_01.c.1014926.de/nachrichten/sachsen-anhalt_wuerde_sich_mit_der_afd_selbst_am_meisten_schaden.html">“AfD paradox”</a>: it is precisely AfD voters and their regions, the column argues, that would pay a particularly high price for the party's policies. But do people with low incomes really vote AfD more often than others? Survey data from the last federal election suggest that income is a rather weak characteristic for explaining who votes AfD.

The data come from the post-election survey of the German Longitudinal Election Study (GLES) for the 2025 federal election, a large representative survey conducted by GESIS shortly after the election. Respondents were asked which party they voted for with their second vote – the vote that decides the strength of the parties in the Bundestag – and how much net income their household has per month in total, after taxes and social security contributions, in brackets from “under €500” to “€10,000 and more”. For the analysis, these brackets are grouped into eight income groups. Included are all respondents who report a party vote and their household income (n = 5,321); the results are weighted to match the population.

## 1. AfD support barely changes with income

The simplest way to approach the question is to look at the AfD's share of the vote in each income group. Here the picture is clear: AfD voting varies only weakly along the income distribution, and it is not even highest in the lowest income group. Among respondents with a monthly household net income below €1,500, the AfD's share is 17 percent – about as high as among respondents with middle and higher incomes, who have several thousand euros more at their disposal each month. The highest share, 18 percent, is found among households with €2,000 to €2,500. Only above €5,000 a month does AfD support drop noticeably, to 10 and finally 7 percent.

One might suspect that this flat line simply blends two different worlds, since the AfD is much stronger in the East, where incomes are lower on average. But the pattern holds when East and West Germany are looked at separately. In the East, AfD support is considerably higher across all income groups, at 22 to 29 percent up to an income of €5,000; in the West, the line is almost flat at 13 to 15 percent. In both parts of the country, support only falls in the two highest income groups.

## 2. Comparing like with like, the income gap shrinks further

Even this breakdown, however, says little about whether income itself makes the difference. People with low incomes differ from others in other respects too, for instance in age, household size, education or the region they live in – and some of these characteristics are themselves closely linked to AfD voting. If we compare respondents who are alike in these respects, using a regression model that takes age, sex, education, household size and East/West into account, the link between income and AfD voting becomes even weaker. The predicted AfD share then ranges between 18 and 10 percent, and the gap between the lowest and the highest income group shrinks from 11.5 to 7.0 percentage points.

What remains of the gap is driven almost entirely by the very top of the income distribution. This becomes visible when low earners – here respondents with less than €2,000 household net income per month – are compared directly with each of the other income groups. Compared with the next income group, the median group or the group just above the average income, they are not significantly more likely to vote AfD, with or without controls. A significant difference only appears in comparison with the highest income group, whose AfD support is around 7 percentage points lower even among otherwise similar respondents.

That income explains so little is not a peculiarity of the 2025 election but in line with a large body of research. Studies based on the German Socio-Economic Panel (SOEP) find that low income, low education, unemployment or a working-class occupation hardly increase the likelihood of supporting the AfD (<a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11577-017-0446-1">Lengfeld 2017</a>; <a href="https://www.diw.de/de/diw_01.c.595136.de/publikationen/soeppapers/2018_0975/afd-unterstuetzer_sind_nicht_abgehaengt__sondern_auslaenderfeindlich.html">Schröder 2018</a>). Low earners identify only slightly more often with the AfD, and those whose income has objectively fallen do not support it more strongly either (<a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/esr/jcab024">Hartmann, Kurz and Lengfeld 2022</a>). Shortly after the party was founded, its supporters even earned above-average incomes, and later about average ones (<a href="https://www.iwkoeln.de/studien/knut-bergmann-matthias-diermeier-judith-niehues-eine-partei-der-sich-ausgeliefert-fuehlenden-durchschnittsverdiener.html">Bergmann, Diermeier and Niehues 2017</a>). Where economic factors do matter, research points less to objective deprivation than to how people perceive their own position: a low subjective social status (<a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-4446.12319">Gidron and Hall 2017</a>), the fear of social decline in the lower middle class (<a href="https://doi.org/10.1177/0010414020912283">Kurer 2020</a>; <a href="https://doi.org/10.1080/13501763.2020.1733636">Engler and Weisstanner 2021</a>) or the feeling of being treated unfairly compared with others (<a href="https://www.wsi.de/fpdf/HBS-009196/p_wsi_studies_42_2025.pdf">Hövermann 2025</a>).

## 3. AfD voters come mostly from the lower and middle middle class

So far, the focus has been on the AfD alone. Whether its income profile is unusual only becomes clear in comparison with other parties – and here, too, the AfD does not stand out among low earners. Its share is stable across most income groups and only falls at very high incomes. The vote for the CDU/CSU and the Greens varies much more with income: both are clearly weaker among low earners and considerably stronger in the top income groups.

The same data can also be read the other way round: instead of asking how each income group voted, we can ask how much each party's voters earn. Seen this way, AfD voters are concentrated in the lower to middle middle class. Around 47 percent of them live in households with less than €3,000 net income per month, while 15 percent live in households with €5,000 or more. In terms of income structure, the AfD electorate thus closely resembles those of the SPD, the BSW and the Left. It has nothing like the distinctive income profile of the Greens and the FDP, of whose voters 37 and 38 percent respectively have at least €5,000 a month at their disposal.

## 4. It is the Left, not the AfD, that draws on low incomes

If any party draws disproportionately on low incomes, it is the Left. Unlike AfD support, Left support is highest among respondents with the lowest incomes – even when comparing respondents who are otherwise alike – and then declines quite steadily: from 16 percent below €1,500 to 4 percent in the highest income group.

The direct comparisons between income groups show the same contrast. Low earners are significantly more likely to vote for the Left not only than the highest income group but already than the median and mean groups. The CDU/CSU shows the reverse pattern: low earners are around 6 to 8 percentage points less likely to vote for it than the median and mean groups, and 19 points less likely than the highest income group. For the AfD, by contrast, only the comparison with the highest income group is significant, as shown above.

## 5. Education matters far more than income

If income explains so little, what does? Among the characteristics considered here, one stands out: formal education. When education and income are estimated jointly in one model, the difference in predicted AfD support between education groups is far greater than between income groups: among respondents with a lower secondary school qualification (Hauptschule) it is 24 percent, among those with the Abitur 5 percent. Support also falls much more evenly as education rises, while along the income distribution it moves within a narrow band between 10 and 18 percent.

## Conclusion

Taken together, the data confirm what research on the AfD has found for years: the AfD is not a party of low earners. Its support is similar across most of the income distribution and only falls among respondents with very high incomes. Unlike for the Left, a low income does not make an AfD vote noticeably more likely once age, education and region are taken into account; formal education is a far stronger predictor.

None of this means, of course, that economic concerns are irrelevant to AfD voting. But they seem to work less through objective material deprivation – poverty, low income or unemployment – than through how people perceive their own situation. In the WSI employee panel, for example, AfD voting differs only slightly between income groups. Among respondents who fear for their standard of living or feel disadvantaged compared with others, however, more than 30 percent vote AfD – regardless of whether their income is low, middle or high (<a href="https://www.wsi.de/fpdf/HBS-009196/p_wsi_studies_42_2025.pdf">Hövermann 2025</a>). Comparative studies, too, show that perceived status loss and the fear of social decline drive support for the populist right more than actual hardship, especially in the lower middle class (<a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-4446.12319">Gidron and Hall 2017</a>; <a href="https://doi.org/10.1177/0010414020912283">Kurer 2020</a>; <a href="https://doi.org/10.1080/13501763.2020.1733636">Engler and Weisstanner 2021</a>). Such grievances can even reach people who are well off: a recent study based on the SOEP shows that respondents who underestimate their position in the income distribution hold stronger populist attitudes, irrespective of their actual income (<a href="https://doi.org/10.31235/osf.io/2s7mc_v1">Albers, Bischof, Kersting and Kosse 2026</a>). This fits the finding that AfD voters are concentrated in the lower to middle middle class rather than among the poorest.

Even this subjective explanation has its limits, however. The most recent study on the question cautions against overstating perceived deprivation as well. Based on SOEP data from 2014 to 2024, Martin Schröder and colleagues find that concern about immigration accounts for about three quarters of the explainable differences in AfD support, while income, education, life satisfaction, financial worries and fear of decline add little (<a href="https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0350403">Schröder, Rehm and Röcke 2026</a>; see also <a href="https://www.diw.de/de/diw_01.c.774792.de/s_11067.html">Lengfeld and Dilger 2018</a>). Whoever wants to understand AfD support should therefore look less at people's bank accounts than at their perceptions of status, fairness and – above all – immigration.
